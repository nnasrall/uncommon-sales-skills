# Frameworks: Competitive Displacement

## Read the incumbent

Start with respect for what they do well. Pretending the incumbent is bad insults the buyer
who chose them. Name three things:
- **Where they are strong.** What the buyer genuinely gets from them today.
- **Where they fall short for this buyer.** Specific to this account's situation, not a generic
  weakness. The gap that matters is the one this buyer feels.
- **The cost of switching.** Migration, retraining, risk, political cost of admitting the last
  choice is not working. This is the real opponent. You are not beating the competitor; you are
  beating the buyer's inertia.

## Sell the approach before the product

The highest-leverage move in a competitive deal happens before the comparison. If you win
agreement on the approach, on "what must be true of a solution for this to work", you shape
the criteria, and a well-shaped criteria list narrows the search to you. Lead the buyer to
define requirements the incumbent structurally cannot meet, and the feature-by-feature bake-off
never has to happen.

## Be different, not better

Better invites a comparison you may lose, because the incumbent is the default and ties go to
the status quo. Different reframes the decision.
- Find 2 to 3 differences in **approach or outcome**, not features. How you work, or what the
  buyer ends up with, that the incumbent's model cannot replicate.
- Change the language. When everyone says the same word, coin a sharper one that reframes the
  category around your strength.
- **Name a villain.** The villain is the status-quo problem the incumbent cannot solve because
  of how they are built. Not the competitor as a company, the structural gap. A named villain
  gives the buyer something to move against.

## The switching story

Buyers do not switch because you are 10 percent better. They switch when the status quo becomes
the risky choice. Tell the story of what has changed, in their business or their market, that
makes standing still the dangerous option.

Frame it with the change equation: dissatisfaction with today, times a clear vision of the
after, times an easy first step, has to beat the resistance to changing. If any of those three
is missing, the deal stalls in "no decision", which is the incumbent's best friend. Your job is
to raise all three and shrink the first step (a small, reversible pilot beats a rip-and-replace).

## Trap-setting, not badmouthing

Never badmouth the competitor. It makes you look small and makes the buyer defensive about
their own past decision. Instead, ask questions that let the buyer surface the gaps themselves.
The buyer's own conclusion is worth ten of your accusations. "Seeing around corners" means
raising the nonobvious risk they have not considered yet. A buyer does not forget the moment you
stopped them from stepping on a landmine, even when it means slowing your own deal to do it right.
